# CEO KPI Explainer
**For non-clinical board members and executives**

Every metric on the dashboard, explained in plain language. Each KPI has:
- **What it is** — the plain-English definition
- **Why it matters** — the business or regulatory implication
- **What "good" looks like** — benchmark ranges
- **What to do if it's bad** — the decision it should trigger

---

## 1. Total Encounters

**What it is.** Every time a patient interacts with the health system — inpatient stay, ER visit, clinic appointment, or observation — it's counted as one encounter. This is the volume signal.

**Why it matters.** Volume drives revenue, staffing needs, and market share. A drop in encounters is often the earliest warning sign of a problem (loss of a payer contract, physician departure, competitor opening nearby).

**What "good" looks like.** Trending upward or stable, with growth outpacing the local population growth rate.

**If it's falling.** Investigate by facility, department, and payer to isolate the cause. Look for provider departures, coding changes, or a shift to outpatient.

---

## 2. Unique Patients

**What it is.** The number of distinct individuals seen — regardless of how many times.

**Why it matters.** A health system with 10,000 encounters from 100 patients is very different from one with 10,000 encounters from 5,000 patients. The first is a chronic-care operation; the second is a population health operation. Both can be valid, but they require very different strategies.

**What "good" looks like.** Depends on business model. Growing unique patients alongside stable encounters-per-patient suggests market expansion. Falling unique patients with rising encounters-per-patient suggests you're serving a shrinking, sicker population.

---

## 3. Total Billed

**What it is.** The gross amount the hospital charges for all services rendered, before any adjustments or payments.

**Why it matters.** Billed dollars are a leading indicator of revenue cycle capacity, but they are **not revenue**. A high billed number with a low collection rate is a red flag — either you're over-charging relative to what payers will pay, or your billing process is broken.

**What "good" looks like.** Grows in proportion to encounter volume and case-mix complexity. Sudden spikes in billed without volume growth usually indicate a coding change, not real revenue growth.

---

## 4. Total Paid (Net Revenue)

**What it is.** The actual money received from payers and patients. This is the number that hits the income statement.

**Why it matters.** This is the metric that determines whether the organization is financially sustainable. Growth in billed without growth in paid is a warning sign of collection breakdown.

**What "good" looks like.** Growing year-over-year, and at least 92–96% of the industry-standard "expected" amount for a given case mix.

---

## 5. Net Collection Rate

**What it is.** Total paid ÷ total billed. The percentage of billed charges that actually convert into cash.

**Why it matters.** It's the single best measure of revenue cycle health. Every point of collection rate improvement is margin that goes straight to the bottom line.

**What "good" looks like.** 92–96% for commercial payers, 85–92% for Medicaid, 90–94% blended. Below 85% indicates a serious collections problem.

**If it's low.** Investigate denial reason codes, payer mix shifts, prior-authorization failures, and coding accuracy. Often a hospital has a single payer or single DRG family dragging the number down.

---

## 6. Denial Rate

**What it is.** The percentage of claims that payers refuse to pay, either entirely or partially. Denials are the leading cause of lost revenue in US healthcare.

**Why it matters.** Every denial is a claim that either needs to be reworked (costing staff time) or written off (costing money). A rising denial rate is often an early warning that a payer is tightening its policies or that coding quality has slipped.

**What "good" looks like.** 4–6% for commercial, 8–12% for Medicaid. Above 15% is a crisis.

**If it's high.** Break it down by payer. If one payer is 3x the rest, that contract needs renegotiation. If denials are concentrated in a single reason code (e.g., medical necessity), it's a clinical documentation issue.

---

## 7. Avg Length of Stay (LOS)

**What it is.** Average number of days a patient spends in the hospital during an inpatient stay.

**Why it matters.** LOS is the primary driver of inpatient cost and bed capacity. A 0.5-day reduction in average LOS across a large hospital frees up hundreds of bed-days per year — the equivalent of a new floor.

**What "good" looks like.** 4.5–5.5 days nationally for adult inpatients, but varies widely by case mix. A hospital with a high-acuity surgical program legitimately runs longer than a community hospital.

**If it's high.** Investigate discharge planning bottlenecks, delayed ancillary test turnaround, and specialty coverage. If it's rising without case-mix change, it's usually a care-coordination problem, not a severity problem.

---

## 8. Median Length of Stay

**What it is.** The middle value of LOS — half of patients stay fewer days, half stay more.

**Why it matters.** The median is less influenced by a few very long outliers than the mean. When mean and median diverge sharply, you have "long-tail" patients — a small number of stays eating a disproportionate share of bed-days. This is a signal that a specific case management intervention is needed.

**What "good" looks like.** Median typically 40–50% of the mean. A mean of 6.0 with a median of 5.0 is normal. Mean of 9.0 with median of 4.0 signals 5–10 outliers costing the hospital millions.

---

## 9. Encounters per Patient

**What it is.** Total encounters ÷ unique patients. Measures how often each patient engages with the system.

**Why it matters.** High ratios suggest a chronic or complex patient population. This can be a strong positive (well-managed chronic care) or a warning (poorly coordinated care causing repeat visits). Look at ED vs outpatient mix to tell the difference.

**What "good" looks like.** 2–4 for a mixed population, higher for chronic-care-focused systems.

---

## 10. Avg Billed per Encounter

**What it is.** Total billed ÷ total encounters.

**Why it matters.** This is a proxy for case-mix intensity. Rising average billed per encounter often means you're treating sicker patients — but if it's rising faster than your case-mix index (CMI), it may indicate coding creep or a compliance risk.

**What "good" looks like.** Grows gradually with case-mix. Sudden jumps warrant a compliance review.

---

## 11. BP Control Rate

**What it is.** Percentage of patients whose most recent blood pressure reading is under 140/90 mmHg — the standard threshold for hypertension control.

**Why it matters.** Blood pressure control is one of the most reliable population-health indicators. Uncontrolled hypertension drives strokes, heart attacks, kidney disease, and ED visits — all of which are expensive. This metric is tracked by CMS, NCQA (HEDIS), and the Joint Commission.

**What "good" looks like.** 70–80% is good. National average is around 65%. Top-performing systems hit 80%+.

**If it's low.** Look for gaps in follow-up (are patients being seen every 3 months?), medication adherence issues, or social barriers (pharmacy access, food insecurity).

---

## 12. Abnormal Lab Rate

**What it is.** Percentage of lab results that fall outside the reference range.

**Why it matters.** A rising abnormal rate can indicate a sicker patient population, delayed care, or a lab quality problem. A falling rate is usually good, but if it's falling due to fewer tests being ordered (rather than better outcomes), that's a hidden access problem.

**What "good" looks like.** 5–15% depending on the test mix. Always analyze per test, not aggregated.

---

## 13. Obesity Rate (BMI ≥ 30)

**What it is.** Percentage of patients with a recorded BMI of 30 or higher.

**Why it matters.** Obesity is the upstream driver of diabetes, hypertension, sleep apnea, joint disease, and dozens of expensive downstream conditions. It's also a key input to population-health risk models.

**What "good" looks like.** Reflects your community. National average is ~42%. If you're seeing rates above your community baseline, your documentation may be more complete than peers — which is actually a good thing for value-based payment.

---

## 14. 30-day Readmission Rate

**What it is.** Percentage of patients who are readmitted to the hospital within 30 days of a prior discharge.

**Why it matters.** Readmissions are the single most scrutinized quality metric in American healthcare. CMS penalizes hospitals financially through the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program. High readmissions also indicate problems with discharge planning, patient education, and post-acute care coordination.

**What "good" looks like.** 12–15% for all-cause readmissions. Below 10% is excellent. Above 20% is a regulatory and financial risk.

**If it's high.** Investigate by DRG (the cohort heatmap tells you which conditions drive it), by payer, and by discharge disposition. Often concentrated in heart failure, COPD, and sepsis — each has a well-defined intervention.

---

## 15. Revenue Cycle Waterfall

**What it is.** A visual breakdown of how billed charges become collected cash, showing three deductions: denials, contractual adjustments, and patient responsibility.

**Why it matters.** This is the single most important board slide for a hospital CFO. It shows where each dollar of billed revenue is lost — and every category has a different fix.

- **High denials** → coding / prior-auth / utilization review problem
- **High contractuals** → payer contract problem
- **High patient responsibility leakage** → front-desk and billing problem

**What "good" looks like.** Denials < 5% of billed, contractuals in line with payer contracts, patient collections > 95% of responsibility.

---

## 16. Equity Gap (LOS or Readmission by Race)

**What it is.** The difference in outcome (e.g., length of stay, readmission rate, BP control) between the best-performing racial/ethnic group and the worst.

**Why it matters.** This is now a regulatory requirement. CMS requires hospitals to stratify quality measures by race, ethnicity, language, and payer. The Joint Commission has new health equity standards. Disparities that persist without an action plan can trigger penalties and accreditation findings.

**What "good" looks like.** A gap close to zero, and **narrowing over time**. The absolute gap matters less than the trend — boards want to see you moving toward equity.

**If it's wide or widening.** Drill into the specific outcome and population. Fund targeted interventions (community health workers, transportation, culturally tailored education). Report progress quarterly.

---

## 17. Encounter Mix

**What it is.** The breakdown of encounters into inpatient, outpatient, emergency, and observation.

**Why it matters.** Each type has different economics. ED visits are usually low-margin or loss-making; surgical inpatients are high-margin; observation is a cost-containment tool. The mix tells you what kind of institution you're running.

**What "good" looks like.** Depends on strategy. A community hospital might be 60% outpatient, 20% ED, 15% inpatient. A surgical specialty hospital would be inverted.

---

## 18. Top Diagnoses

**What it is.** The most frequent conditions treated in the system.

**Why it matters.** This is the demand signal for population-health investment. If diabetes is #1, you need a diabetes program. If COPD is #2, you need pulmonary rehab. Chronic conditions also drive value-based payment performance.

**What to do with it.** Cross-reference with the readmission heatmap. The diagnoses that are both high-volume **and** high-readmission are your highest-ROI intervention targets.

---

## 19. Facility Comparison

**What it is.** Side-by-side volume, LOS, quality, and financial performance of each hospital site.

**Why it matters.** Best-practice sharing starts with visibility. If one site has a 4.0-day LOS and another has a 6.5-day LOS for the same case mix, the difference is worth millions — and the fix is usually transferable.

**What "good" looks like.** All sites within 15% of each other on core metrics. Wide gaps indicate either case-mix differences (defensible) or operational variance (not defensible).

---

## Summary — the CEO's three questions

Every board meeting should answer three questions with this dashboard:

1. **Are we financially healthy?** → Net Collection Rate, Denial Rate, Waterfall
2. **Are we delivering quality care?** → BP Control, Readmission Rate, LOS
3. **Are we serving our community equitably?** → Equity Trend, Encounter Mix, Top Diagnoses

If any of those three is red, it's a board-level agenda item.
